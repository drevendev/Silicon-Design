# Character and attachment

We want products people can get attached to. Clean layout, readable text and working controls create useful conditions; they do not supply a product's personality. Character comes from coherent choices that belong to a particular product, encountered in actual use.

This chapter is our synthesis of the [source analysis](../research/deep-read.md). Its exercises guide design and critique. They are not validated predictors of affection, retention or commercial success.

## Give the feeling a situation

Specify who should feel what, when, and why. “Playful” is too broad. A game can be curious during exploration, precise while comparing costs, and celebratory after a difficult discovery. An operations tool may feel reassuring because an error preserves work and explains recovery.

Use this short contract:

| Prompt | Useful answer shape |
| --- | --- |
| Person and moment | Someone returning to a half-finished task after an interruption |
| Desired feeling | Oriented and in control |
| Visual expression | Their active work remains the dominant object; supporting chrome recedes |
| Behavioral expression | Position and unsent input survive; changes since the last visit are legible |
| Voice | A concise explanation of what resumed and what still needs attention |
| Boundary | No invented activity, forced celebration or surprise navigation |

These are illustrative decisions, not requirements to add persistence to every project. Implement features only when they belong to the authorized scope.

## Find material that belongs to the product

Gather concrete inputs: a real artifact the product creates, a useful diagram from its subject, language its audience uses, physical materials, authored imagery, a characteristic action. Ask what each reference teaches about hierarchy, pace, material or tone. Record what will be different in our product.

A reference set made entirely of popular SaaS homepages tends to reproduce their shared conventions. Include product evidence and references from the subject itself. A restoration journal might draw on condition reports and repair marks; a game might draw on the material transformation the player performs. These are directions to investigate, not permission to invent historical facts or copy assets.

Select a recognizable idea and carry it through the requested surfaces: the primary task, difficult state and completion when those are present. For a static frame, demonstrate it in that frame; do not invent workflows or features to fill the exercise. It can be typographic, spatial, illustrative, verbal or behavioral. Repetition should communicate a consistent idea, with variation appropriate to the moment. A logo repeated in every corner is not enough.

## Compose intensity and rhythm

Choose how assertively the product greets people, how much its composition varies, and how much motion helps the experience. Describe those choices in words tied to the task. A quiet editor can have a strong identity. Expressiveness does not require maximal size, saturation or animation.

Make at least one hierarchy decision with conviction: a meaningful scale contrast, a deliberately dominant artifact, a crop that reveals the subject, or supporting information that visibly recedes. Give the experience changes in pace where the content calls for them. Predictable rows may be exactly right for a queue; a sequence of identical sections can flatten a story.

When a journey is in scope, inspect it beyond the opening. A dramatic introduction followed by interchangeable cards loses its own direction. Arbitrary alternation also creates noise. Rhythm should help the person read, compare, anticipate and finish.

## Write a voice that can handle trouble

Voice stays recognizable; tone responds to context. Define a few real examples before declaring the product “friendly.” Write the same product in three situations: invitation, success and failure.

| Situation | A useful writing decision | A common failure |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Invitation | Name the thing someone can make, inspect or change | A broad “Get started” with no clear consequence |
| Success | Confirm the actual result using the same vocabulary as the action | Vague praise that leaves the state uncertain |
| Failure | Explain what happened, what remains safe and the next useful action | A joke, blame, an obscure code or a mascot performing distress |

Keep deliberate quirks that suit the audience. Do not turn every label into a joke or every confirmation into praise. Clarity is compatible with personality; performative friendliness can make the product feel less considerate.

## Put care into repeated use

Look for a moment where the interface can show it understands the task. Useful examples include retaining a comparison while details open, providing an exact value behind a compact number, revealing a shortcut when relevant, or distinguishing a finished inbox from a connection failure.

These details create opportunities for trust and appreciation. The appropriate detail depends on the product. A quiet, immediate action may be more satisfying than an elaborate response. A celebratory animation needs a reason to replay; frequently used controls should stay efficient.

Treat first use, routine use and returning after an absence separately. Help should appear near the feature it explains and remain dismissible. An experienced person should not repeatedly pay the cost of a new-user tour. When user-controlled memory or customization belongs in the product, make its behavior understandable and reversible.

## Use memorable moments with restraint

A moment worth remembering might be the way a game-world object changes after crafting, a characteristic transition that explains where a saved item went, or a completion message whose words belong to this product. It need not be an animated gimmick.

Check the cost: attention, time, motion sensitivity, asset weight, repetition and loss of clarity. Keep an alternative for necessary feedback without motion or sound. Never obstruct a task, manufacture urgency, or guilt someone into returning in the name of attachment.

## Run two different reviews

**Usefulness review:** can the intended person understand and complete the task, read the information, operate the controls and recover from failure? Record direct checks and limits.

**Character review:** point to the choices that make the product specific and coherent. Examine composition, imagery, voice and behavior together. Check whether the memorable idea survives outside the introduction and whether the repeated experience still feels considerate. Record critique as judgment or a hypothesis unless audience observations support more.

Try these exercises:

- Replace the name with an unrelated product. Identify exactly what must change. A reusable button should survive; the product's main story and artifacts usually should not.
- Temporarily normalize the palette and typography. Look for identity in content, relationships and behavior. This probes dependence on a fashionable skin; it is not a requirement to ship without expressive type or color.
- Walk through a tenth visit. Find repeated instructions, waiting, praise or motion that now gets in the way.
- Ask a relevant person what they remember and how they would describe it. Record their actual response. Do not claim a recall test happened because an agent can produce a memorable sentence.

A result can pass the first review and fall short in the second. Our [initial specimens](../research/specimen-critique.md) demonstrate why both are needed. Keep useful engineering work and reopen the weak design decisions.
